# 04 — Location geography and plates

A production location is not merely a pretty background. Actors must be able to move
inside it, the camera must have a repeatable axis, light needs a motivated source, and
later views must preserve the same visible geography.

## Build the location contract first

Record these before generating a production plate:

- story function and emotional pressure of the place;
- entrances, exits, obstacles, playable routes, and elevation changes;
- stable foreground, midground, and background anchors;
- actor start/end marks and important eyelines;
- primary screen axis and the conditions for crossing it;
- motivated light sources, direction, falloff, and time state;
- material and color relationships from the visual bible;
- which geography may remain unknown without breaking a later cut.

Draw a simple top-down map when two or more shots depend on spatial continuity. A text
prompt cannot substitute for a missing spatial decision.

For a long dialogue or ensemble scene, maintain a continuity ledger in the location
map: seat/standing mark, screen-side owner, eyeline pair, depth order, foreground
occluder, and every allowed reposition. Each shot may show a different crop, but it
must resolve to the same ledger unless the scene records the move that changed it.

When complex staging repeatedly drifts, invoke the optional production-control export
from `timed-storyboard`: a labeled review map and a separate clean geometry-only map.
It is subordinate to this location authority and never carries faces, wardrobe,
materials, lighting, or style.

## Choose coverage from the scene

Do not force a universal 3/4 master and reverse. Choose the minimum views that prove the
planned blocking and edit:

- a single plate can be enough when no later cut claims unseen geography;
- a master plus reverse is useful when eyelines and opposing screen direction matter;
- an entrance, exit, or object interaction may require a dedicated axis view;
- a moving shot needs visible path clearance and depth anchors along the route.

Each additional view is a continuity test. Compare openings, fixed objects, scale,
materials, light side, horizon, and actor routes before treating it as the same place.

## Distinct scenes need distinct places

Reuse a location only when the story intentionally returns there. A shared genre,
palette, haze, lens, or lighting recipe is not enough to make different scenes feel
authored. For every new scene, name at least one unique spatial structure, one unique
material relationship, and one unique light or weather state that expresses its beat.
Do not fill a near-future world with generic neon, holograms, observatory equipment,
fog, or industrial clutter unless the story requires them.

## Plate approval

Approve the exact intended crop only when the required geography is readable and the
planned action can occur without invention. Inspect:

1. openings, routes, scale, and depth planes;
2. shadows and motivated light direction;
3. broken railings, furniture, signs, or repeating background structures;
4. material transitions, reflections, haze, and distant detail;
5. negative space needed for actors, props, camera motion, and reframing.

Oversharpened or oily surfaces often crawl in motion. Uniform sharpness across every
plane destroys depth. Both are source failures, not problems to disguise later with
camera movement.

If a required optical characteristic is not retained reliably by the active video
route, run a controlled A/B capability test. Only then may an approved location plate
carry that visible optical result as part of its pixels. Record the plate and hash as
the carrier; do not assume this workaround applies to another model, account, crop, or
camera move, and do not use metadata words as proof that the optics survived.

## Repair boundary

Prefer a fresh source generation when geography, perspective, overall light, or image
quality is wrong. Use a patch only for a genuinely local defect on an otherwise
approved plate. Always patch from the immutable source and composite only the accepted
region; never re-edit the prior derivative.

Model routing comes from `references/03-models-and-proof.md` and current provider
evidence. Do not assume one named model is always the first edit route.
