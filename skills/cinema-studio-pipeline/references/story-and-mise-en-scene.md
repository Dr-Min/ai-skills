# Story and mise-en-scene contract

Use this reference when a film is visually attractive but dramatically empty, when a
new scene is being planned, or when the result feels like unrelated AI images.

## Story pressure

Resolve cause, present goal, obstacle, stakes, discovery, choice, and visible
consequence. Give every scene a before/after change. A movement, location change,
costume change, or style transition must be caused by a story event.

Before boarding a scene, run a five-part stress test: objective, obstacle, tactic,
reversal, and value shift. Mark each as `PASS`, `WEAK`, or `MISSING`, identify the
weakest causal link, and propose the smallest repair before considering a rewrite.
For ensemble scenes, distinguish the one external event everyone experiences from
each person's separate physical response. This prevents four characters from acting
as one synchronized emotional unit.

## Visual system

Translate the story into repeatable visible rules:

- world and period, including contamination that invalidates a take;
- public image versus private self;
- dominant materials, surface age, and motivated practical light;
- palette hierarchy and the story reason for an accent color;
- lens and camera grammar by information type;
- recurring object, threshold, moon, reflection, damage, or other motif;
- how the frame becomes emptier, tighter, warmer, colder, faster, or stiller as the
  character changes.

## Mise-en-scene per shot

State what foreground, midground, background, architecture, props, bodies, light, and
negative space reveal about power and choice. Do not use the background as decoration.
Make entrances, exits, distance changes, stopped hand activity, thresholds, and object
placement carry story.

## Cut function

Every cut must reveal information, reaction, geography, action consequence, contrast,
memory, or an intentional music event. Pair large action with a human reaction. Hold
the emotional tail after a line or event. Reject a beautiful shot that has no role in
the sequence.
